# Editorial review

Review the changed writing before polishing sentences. Start from the intended reader and the actual question the piece answers. Read the neighboring pieces and contributor guidance if available.

1. Summarize the argument in one sentence. If it merely repeats another article, identify what is missing rather than proposing a new slogan.
2. Follow the example from beginning to end. Check whether each choice has a reason, whether the consequence follows, and where the argument would fail. Hypothetical examples must be identifiable as such; do not invent anecdotes, results, or citations.
3. Mark claims that need external evidence or narrower wording. Keep a promise, an observation, a hypothesis, and a design choice distinct. Verify links and quotes if the sources are accessible; label anything you cannot check.
4. Read for rhythm and clarity. Prefer the detail that lets a reader picture the work. Cut repetitive summaries, automatic contrasts, grand claims, and paragraphs that could apply to any topic. Do not enforce arbitrary word bans or punctuation quotas. Preserve the author's voice.
5. End with the smallest useful changes, ordered by impact. Cite the passage or heading, explain the problem, and offer a concrete repair. Leave sentences alone when they already work.

## Example

"The team unlocks unprecedented trust and accelerates future delivery" makes two unsupported promises. If the example only shows that a fresh engineer can find and run a replay, say so; explain what the replay does not establish.

Report findings before rewriting. If asked to revise, make the changes and reread the whole affected passage in context. Report checked sources and remaining uncertainties separately. A green site build does not demonstrate a good essay.
