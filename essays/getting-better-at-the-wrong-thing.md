# Getting better at the wrong thing

*Knowing when to improve a process and when to question it*

Suppose a billing team spends every Friday reconciling a spreadsheet with the account ledger. They have become good at it. An agent prepares the export, another script highlights disagreements, and a senior engineer signs off the result. The weekly job takes less time than it used to.

Then someone asks why the spreadsheet exists. It was introduced when support could not see pending credits. Support has had that view for a year. The reconciliation still catches some real discrepancies, but the original reason for sending the spreadsheet has disappeared.

The next investment could improve the export again. It could also preserve the useful discrepancy check and retire the handoff. The team will not discover the second possibility by measuring export speed alone.

This is an invented example, but it exposes a weakness in any improvement method: becoming better at a task can distract us from asking whether the task still serves its purpose.

## Success gives the familiar work an advantage

James March's [Exploration and Exploitation in Organizational Learning](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2.1.71) describes a tension between refining what an organization already knows and searching for new possibilities. His models show how learning that improves exploitation more quickly can support short-term performance while damaging longer-term adaptation.

The tension is easy to recognize in planning. The export team can estimate the benefit of a faster comparison script. Investigating a different support workflow has a less certain result, and its benefit might appear in another team's work. The known improvement arrives with a clearer justification, even when the unfamiliar question matters more.

The Theory of Constraints helps identify what limits a chosen outcome. It still depends on choosing the outcome and the system sensibly. If we define the job as producing Friday's spreadsheet, a faster export looks useful. If the job is helping customers understand their credits, we should examine whether the spreadsheet belongs in it.

## Ask what the rule protects

Chris Argyris's [double-loop learning](https://store.hbr.org/product/double-loop-learning-in-organizations/77502) examines learning that changes governing assumptions, rather than only correcting actions within them. For the billing team, the question expands from how to reconcile the spreadsheet to why reconciliation has this form and who still needs its result.

This does not make established procedures guilty until proven innocent. The sign-off may catch a risk nobody has documented well. A seemingly redundant check may support an obligation elsewhere. Ask the people doing and receiving the work, inspect recent cases, and find the authority responsible for changing it. The useful outcome might be a narrower rule with a clearer reason.

AI can collect the history and propose explanations. It can also make an obsolete rule easier to obey, or give an old decision new authority by summarizing it confidently. Keep the source, its date and its conditions visible. A tidy answer is a reason to inspect the evidence, especially when the people who made the original decision are no longer around.

## Fund a question with a possible answer

Exploration need not begin with a new product team or an architecture rewrite. The billing team could observe support handling a few credit enquiries, trace what information they use, and try a rough version of a more direct workflow. The question is whether support can resolve the relevant enquiries with less effort and adequate evidence.

State what would change the decision before preparing the prototype. If support still needs the spreadsheet for cases the new view cannot explain, that is useful evidence. If the new view handles ordinary credits but hides disputed ones, the next attempt should expose that distinction. Producing several polished alternatives would not answer it.

Cheaper implementation may make such experiments affordable. The scarce part can move to recruiting participants, understanding their work or deciding what an observation means. Synthetic customers can help rehearse an interview or find holes in a prototype. They cannot tell us how actual customers behave.

Three investment purposes help keep the choice visible:

- **Exploitation:** improve work the team already understands, such as the ledger discrepancy check.
- **Exploration:** investigate a different approach or opportunity, such as resolving enquiries without the weekly export.
- **Preservation:** retain an ability that may be needed under different conditions, such as diagnosing a discrepancy when the usual automation is unavailable.

These are purposes, not mandatory budget percentages. One investigation may serve several. Judge an exploration by whether it resolves a consequential uncertainty; judging it only by delivered features encourages people to disguise uncertainty as a commitment. Set a spending limit and a decision date so that exploration can also end.

## Change what the organization acts on

If the export is retired, deleting its scheduled job is only part of the change. Support instructions, onboarding exercises and agent guidance may still send people toward it. The organization has to revise the knowledge that governs its behavior.

For consequential guidance, keep enough context to answer four questions: what supports it, where it applies, who can revise it and what change would make it suspect. A rule tied to a particular provider should be reviewed when that provider changes. A customer assumption should be revisited when observed behavior contradicts it. A calendar reminder alone is a weak substitute for noticing those events.

Some old material should remain as history. Label it so it cannot quietly become current advice again. The [Verified retrieval pattern](https://grove.github.io/patterns-of-compounding-engineering/patterns/verified-retrieval.html) offers a practical check: give a fresh engineer or agent the new question and see what they find and apply. That tests the route to the answer as well as the document.

The team should also preserve the expertise that still matters. Removing the spreadsheet does not remove the need to understand the ledger. A safe rehearsal with an unfamiliar discrepancy can show whether people can still investigate without relying on the retired workflow.

The next Friday is a useful review point. Did support get the information it needed? Did the discrepancy check still catch the relevant cases? Who had to do unexpected work? The answers determine whether the change helped. A shorter checklist, like a faster export, is only an intermediate result.
